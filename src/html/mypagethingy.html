How we can solve it.

There are numerous approaches to solving the problem. A most remarkable one would be the use of nuclear energy. Not many people are aware of the positive use of nuclear energy, as its reputation has been sullied by tragedies like Chornobyl. However, it has been proven multiple times on a scientific level to bring positive results and less pollutant emissions. For example, the nuclear power plant in France provides about 70% of the country's electricity with minimal carbon emissions.

There are many benefits to using nuclear energy, including:

  • It is a clean source of energy.
    • It produces carbon-free air emissions.
    • It can power multiple communities for years, for example: it powers more than 28 communities in the U.S. alone.
  • It is a reliable source of energy. It can only harm people if not properly managed.
  • It is a cost-effective source of energy - it has lower operational costs compared to fossil fuels.

History of Nuclear Energy.

The history of nuclear energy spans foundational scientific discoveries, wartime weapons development, and the transition to peaceful power generation.

1. Scientific Discoveries (1895–1938)

  • In the very beginning, Wilhelm Roentgen discovered X-rays in 1895, and Henri Becquerel discovered natural radioactivity in uranium in 1896. Marie and Pierre Curie soon isolated radioactive elements like polonium and radium. [1] World Nuclear Association

  • The Atomic Structure: James Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932, providing a key tool to probe the atomic nucleus. [1] World Nuclear Association

  • Nuclear Fission: In 1938, German chemists Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann discovered uranium fission, which Lise Meitner and Otto Robert Frisch later explained and named. [1] World Nuclear Association

2. Wartime Research and the Manhattan Project (1939–1945)

  • Chain Reactions: Enrico Fermi led the team that achieved the first man-made, self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction at Chicago Pile-1 on December 2, 1942. [1] IAEA, [2] World Nuclear Association

  • Military Application: Under the Manhattan Project, the United States developed uranium-235 and plutonium-239 atomic bombs, culminating in the Trinity test and the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in 1945. The Soviet Union tested its first atomic device in 1949. [1] What Is Nuclear?, [2] World Nuclear Association

3. Dawn of Civil Nuclear Power (1951–1970s)

  • First Electricity: The Experimental Breeder Reactor I (EBR-I) in Idaho produced the world's first usable nuclear electricity on December 20, 1951. [1] Energy.Gov, [2] IAEA.org

  • Grid Connection: The Soviet Union's Obninsk nuclear power plant became the first to supply electricity to a national grid in June 1954. [1] Wikipedia, [2] IAEA.org

  • Commercial Expansion: The United Kingdom opened Calder Hall in 1956, and the United States launched its first commercial pressurized water reactor at Shippingport, Pennsylvania, in 1957. [1] Department of Energy, [2] Science Museum, [3] World Nuclear Association

  • Atoms for Peace: President Dwight D. Eisenhower's 1953 initiative promoted civilian nuclear technology, leading to the creation of the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) in 1957. Global reactor deployment surged notably after the 1970s oil crises. [1] Energy.Gov, [2] World-nuclear.org

4. Safety Crises and Modern Revival (1979–Present)

  • Major Incidents: Major accidents—including Three Mile Island (1979), Chernobyl (1986), and Fukushima Daiichi (2011)—slowed nuclear growth and triggered phase-outs in countries like Italy and Germany.
  • Decarbonization Era: In the 21st century, nuclear energy experienced a renewed focus driven by energy security, plant life extensions, rapid expansion in Asian nations like China and India, and its role in achieving net-zero carbon goals. [1] World Nuclear Association, [2] Wikipedia, [3] What Is Nuclear?
  • More details are available via the World Nuclear Association Outline History of Nuclear Energy and the Department of Energy History Page. [1] World Nuclear Association, [2]Department of Energy

Conclusion.

In conclusion, nuclear energy, just like any kind of energy, can only be destructive if safety measures are neglected. Nuclear energy offers a promising solution to the world's energy needs while minimizing environmental impact. With continued advancements in technology and safety measures, nuclear power can play a crucial role in creating a more sustainable future.
